Virtual Assets Act 2026: Pakistan's Crypto Law, Explained Simply

For eight years, the honest answer to "is crypto legal in Pakistan?" was a shrug. In March 2026, parliament answered it. Here is the new law in plain English.

The short version

Pakistan's parliament passed the Virtual Assets Act 2026, which came into force on 5 March 2026. It is the country's first comprehensive legal framework for digital assets. It creates a dedicated regulator — the Pakistan Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (PVARA) — and gives it the power to license and supervise every crypto business operating in Pakistan.

How we got here

  • 2018: The State Bank of Pakistan ordered banks and payment companies to stay away from virtual currencies. Not a criminal ban on owning crypto — but a banking blockade.
  • 2018–2025: Pakistanis bought crypto anyway. Chainalysis ranked Pakistan 3rd globally in its 2025 adoption index, and 2nd for retail activity on centralised exchanges.
  • July 2025: A presidential ordinance formally established PVARA as an autonomous federal body.
  • March 2026: Parliament passed the Virtual Assets Act, putting the framework on permanent footing.
  • April 2026: The State Bank replaced its 2018 prohibition — banks may now serve PVARA-licensed crypto firms.
  • August 2026: PVARA notified licensing regulations and opened its application portal.

What the Act does

  • Licences businesses, not users. Exchanges, custodians, wallet operators, lenders, brokers, advisers and token issuers need PVARA licences. Individual holders are not the target.
  • Sets real penalties. Operating without a licence: up to 5 years' prison and/or Rs 50 million fine. Unauthorised token offerings: up to 3 years and/or Rs 25 million.
  • Fights financial crime. The framework brings AML and counter-terror-financing powers in line with international standards.
  • Writes Shariah into the statute. A Shariah Advisory Committee sits inside PVARA; firms offering Shariah-compliant products must follow its rulings.

What the Act does NOT do

  • It does not make Bitcoin legal tender. The Act's own definitions rule that out. Crypto is a regulated asset, not money.
  • It does not let banks trade crypto. Banks may bank licensed firms, but cannot trade or hold crypto with their own or depositors' money.
  • It does not settle the tax question by itself. A 20–30% capital gains tax on crypto profits was discussed ahead of Budget 2026-27 — confirm the enacted treatment with the FBR or a tax professional before filing. (See our complete tax guide.)

Why it matters

Pakistan is the world's third-largest crypto market by adoption — built almost entirely outside the formal financial system. The Act is the state's attempt to pull that activity into daylight: licensed rails, banked firms, protected consumers. Whether it succeeds depends on execution — but the direction of travel is now written in law.
