Pakistan Opens Crypto Licensing Portal: What It Means for You

The Pakistan Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority has notified full licensing regulations and opened its application portal — ending nearly a decade of regulatory grey area for crypto businesses in the country.

B
BlockPak Research Desk
6 min read • English
Share:

On 21 August 2026, PVARA chairman Bilal Bin Saqib announced the notification of the Licensing Regulations and the opening of the licensing portal — the operational go-live of the Virtual Assets Act 2026, which parliament passed in March and which came into force on 5 March 2026.

"For approximately a decade, Pakistan's answer to virtual assets was complete permission and complete ban," Saqib said, inviting international crypto companies to "get licensed, get compliant" and build in Pakistan under what he described as clear, public and enforceable rules.

What the regulations cover

PVARA has stood up ten licence categories covering the full virtual-asset business stack:

  • Exchanges and trading platforms
  • Custody services
  • Brokerage
  • Advisory services
  • Lending and borrowing
  • Derivatives
  • Asset management
  • Transfer and settlement
  • Token issuance
  • Mining-related services

The compliance clock is ticking

Firms that were already serving Pakistani users when the Act took effect were given a window to file for a No-Objection Certificate — widely reported as closing on 5 September 2026 — or cease operations. Running an unlicensed crypto service can now mean up to five years' imprisonment or a fine of up to Rs 50 million (approximately $179,000).

Unauthorized token offerings carry a separate penalty of up to Rs 25 million and three years' imprisonment.

A Shariah-first framework

In a globally unusual move, the Act mandates a Shariah Advisory Committee inside PVARA, and licensed firms offering Shariah-compliant products must follow its rulings. In a market where religious permissibility drives household financial behaviour, this is not a footnote — it is a structural feature of Pakistan's regime.

What this means for ordinary users

  • Owning crypto is not an offence. The Act regulates businesses and token issuers — not individual holders.
  • Crypto is not legal tender. The law says so plainly. You cannot be forced to accept it as payment.
  • Prefer licensed platforms. Over time, PVARA licensing becomes the trust signal — the equivalent of a bank's SBP licence. Unlicensed platforms now carry legal risk for their operators.
  • Banking gets easier. Since April 2026, the State Bank allows banks to open accounts for PVARA-licensed firms — the on-ramp plumbing the industry always lacked.

The bottom line

Pakistan built a top-three global crypto market with no rulebook. Now the rulebook exists, the regulator is staffed, and the portal is open. The next twelve months will show whether licensing delivers what it promises: consumer protection without killing the innovation that made Pakistan a crypto heavyweight in the first place.
